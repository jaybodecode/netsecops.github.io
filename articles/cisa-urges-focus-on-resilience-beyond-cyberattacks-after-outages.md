# CISA Head: Recent Tech Outages Highlight Need for Broader Resilience

**Severity:** informational | **Category:** Policy and Compliance,Security Operations,Incident Response | **Updated:** 2026-11-18 | **Reading time:** 3 min

Following a series of disruptive technology outages, including a global Microsoft Outlook failure and airline ground stops, CISA's acting director, Nick Andersen, stated the events underscore the need for building resilient systems. He argued that the focus should be on withstanding any disruption, whether it's a malicious cyber incident or a technical failure like a bad patch.

## Executive Summary
Speaking at the FedTalks conference on November 17, 2026, Nick Andersen, the acting director of the **[Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)](https://www.cisa.gov)**, highlighted that recent widespread technology failures underscore the urgent need for operational resilience. Pointing to events like global **[Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/security)** Outlook outages and airline ground stops, Andersen argued that the ultimate goal of security and IT operations should be to ensure the continuity of essential services, regardless of the cause of disruption. His comments signal a strategic emphasis from CISA on building systems that are resilient by design, capable of withstanding not only malicious cyberattacks but also technical glitches, bad patches, and other non-malicious failures.

---

## Policy Details
Andersen's remarks outline a strategic perspective for government and industry, shifting the focus from a singular obsession with cyberattack prevention to a broader concept of operational resilience. He stated that from a national security standpoint, the outcome—the continuous delivery of services—is more important than the specific cause of an outage. "Ultimately being able to prevail by being able to demonstrate resilience against disruptions, whether they occur because of things like an IT outage or a bad patch... or because of a malicious cyber incident—we want to make sure we're engineering in the resilience," Andersen said.

This philosophy encourages organizations to design and build systems with the assumption that failures will happen. The key is not just to prevent incidents but to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to them.

## Affected Organizations
Andersen's message is directed at all organizations responsible for critical infrastructure and essential services, including federal agencies, state and local governments, and private sector companies in sectors like technology, finance, and transportation.

## Policy Requirements
This is not a formal regulation but strategic guidance. It calls for organizations to integrate resilience into their system architecture and risk management frameworks. This includes:
-   **Designing for Failure:** Implementing redundancy, failover capabilities, and graceful degradation in systems.
-   **Comprehensive Response Plans:** Developing and testing incident response plans that cover a wide range of scenarios, not just cyberattacks.
-   **Focus on Outcomes:** Measuring success by the ability to maintain critical functions during a disruption, rather than just by the number of attacks blocked.

## Impact Assessment
This strategic guidance from CISA encourages a more mature and holistic approach to risk management. By focusing on resilience, organizations can become more robust and less fragile. The impact for citizens and customers is more reliable services, whether it's the ability to access bank accounts, board a flight, or receive clean drinking water. Andersen noted that the public's primary concern is the outcome, not the technical reason for a failure. This approach moves beyond the "vulnpocalypse" mindset to address the fundamental reliability of the digital infrastructure society depends on.

## Compliance Guidance
To align with CISA's vision for resilience, organizations should:
1.  **Conduct Business Impact Analysis (BIA):** Identify critical business processes and the technology systems that support them.
2.  **Assume Failure:** In system design, assume components will fail and build in mechanisms for recovery and continuity. This includes robust backup and restoration capabilities, a key tenet of the **[D3FEND Restore](https://d3fend.mitre.org/tactic/d3f:Restore)** category.
3.  **Test for Resilience:** Go beyond traditional penetration testing. Conduct chaos engineering experiments, simulate outages of critical components, and test the organization's ability to recover within its Recovery Time Objectives (RTOs).
4.  **Integrate IT and Security:** Foster collaboration between IT operations and cybersecurity teams to ensure that resilience is a shared goal, covering both security incidents and operational failures.
